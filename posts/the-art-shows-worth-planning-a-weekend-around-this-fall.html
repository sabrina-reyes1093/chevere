The Art Shows Worth Planning a Weekend Around This Fall

By Chévere · Aug 23, 2026

From Mary Cassatt in Chicago to Monet in Paris, these exhibitions are good enough to build the trip around.

There are trips you take because you found cheap airfare.

And then there are trips that begin with one very specific thought: I need to see that.

Fall is particularly good at creating the second kind. Museums unveil major exhibitions, cities become infinitely more walkable, restaurant reservations feel exciting again, and suddenly spending an afternoon looking at paintings followed by a very long dinner seems like the ideal itinerary.

This fall's exhibition calendar gives us plenty of excuses.

Mary Cassatt: After Impressionism Art Institute of Chicago · Chicago September 6, 2026–January 3, 2027

Chicago in early fall is already an easy sell. Add a major Mary Cassatt exhibition and the weekend essentially plans itself.

Mary Cassatt: After Impressionism looks beyond the artist's familiar association with Impressionism to explore the evolution of her work and the fiercely independent career behind it. Cassatt trained in Philadelphia before moving through Europe and eventually becoming the only American to exhibit with the French Impressionists.

Make the exhibition your Saturday afternoon anchor, then spend the rest of the weekend somewhere between Michigan Avenue, a good pasta reservation, and one last drink you definitely didn't need.

Make a weekend of it: Art Institute, Millennium Park, drinks somewhere cozy, dinner in West Loop.

Krasner and Pollock: Past Continuous The Metropolitan Museum of Art · New York City October 4, 2026–January 31, 2027

Jackson Pollock may be the household name, but this exhibition places Lee Krasner firmly beside him rather than behind him.

The Met's Krasner and Pollock: Past Continuous brings together more than 120 works and traces both artists' careers in parallel, examining the ways their lives and practices intersected while giving each artist room to exist independently. The museum calls it the first major New York presentation devoted to either artist in more than two decades.

It's exactly the kind of exhibition that gives you an excuse to go to New York without pretending you needed one.

See it in the morning, walk through Central Park afterward, and spend the evening somewhere downtown discussing which artist deserved more attention all along.

Make a weekend of it: The Met, Central Park, bookstore wandering, martinis downtown.

Brancusi: The Artist and His Studio MoMA · New York City October 25, 2026–February 27, 2027

If one major exhibition isn't enough to get you onto a plane, New York has another.

MoMA's fall Brancusi exhibition reconstructs something more intimate than a traditional retrospective: the world surrounding the work.

Constantin Brancusi treated his Paris studio as much more than a room where sculptures were made. It became part workshop, part home, part exhibition space, and eventually something resembling an artwork of its own. For this exhibition, rarely loaned sculptures from the Centre Pompidou will travel to New York alongside photographs, films, furniture, tools, archival material, and works from MoMA's own collection.

For anyone fascinated by how creative people actually live and work—not simply what ends up on the museum pedestal—this is the one.

Make a weekend of it: MoMA, lunch somewhere in SoHo, a gallery or two in Chelsea, dinner you booked three weeks too late.

From the Louvre: Masterpieces of Islamic Art Smithsonian's National Museum of Asian Art · Washington, D.C. September 26, 2026–January 18, 2027

Washington doesn't always get included in the romantic-weekend conversation, which is precisely why it can be so good.

This fall, the Smithsonian's National Museum of Asian Art will present 26 works of Islamic art from the Louvre's collection in Paris.

It's a chance to see objects normally housed across the Atlantic while taking advantage of something D.C. does better than almost anywhere else: an absurd concentration of museums within walking distance of one another.

Give yourself the afternoon. Museum fatigue is real, and trying to conquer the entire Smithsonian system in one weekend is the cultural equivalent of ordering everything on the menu.

Make a weekend of it: National Museum of Asian Art, National Gallery, a long Georgetown walk, dinner somewhere worth dressing for.

Renoir and Love The National Gallery · London October 3, 2026–January 31, 2027

If autumn in London needed another selling point, here it is.

Renoir and Love brings together more than 50 works focusing on affection, flirtation, friendship, courtship, café culture, theater, and social life during a pivotal period in Pierre-Auguste Renoir's career.

Among them is Bal du moulin de la Galette, traveling from the Musée d'Orsay and being exhibited in the UK for the first time. The National Gallery says it will be the country's most significant Renoir exhibition in two decades.

A show centered around people falling in love, talking in cafés, dancing, and spending afternoons together feels almost suspiciously suited to London in October.

Lean into it.

Make a weekend of it: National Gallery, Covent Garden, a pub you found accidentally, Sunday at a market.

Monet, Painting Time Musée de l'Orangerie · Paris September 30, 2026–January 25, 2027

Some exhibitions are worth seeing because of what is on the walls.

Others become even better because of where you're seeing them.

At the Musée de l'Orangerie—already home to Monet's monumental Water Lilies—Monet, Painting Time examines the artist's fascination with capturing a single subject across changing moments, seasons, and light.

The exhibition looks at the evolution that produced Monet's famous series of haystacks, poplars, Rouen Cathedral paintings, and ultimately the Nymphéas that occupied the final decades of his life.

Paris. Monet. Fall.

Sometimes we don't need to overcomplicate things.

Make a weekend of it: L'Orangerie, Tuileries Garden, Saint-Germain wandering, wine, bread, repeat.

Let the Exhibition Be the Excuse

You don't have to be someone who “knows about art” to plan a trip around it.

Choose one exhibition that genuinely makes you curious. Book the ticket. Find the neighborhood restaurant. Walk somewhere afterward. Let everything else happen around it.

The best cultural weekends aren't about checking ten important things off a list.

They're about seeing one thing you remember.
