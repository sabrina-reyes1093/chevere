8 Films That Are Worth Putting Your Phone Down For

By Chévere · Aug 8, 2026

No second-screening. No casual scrolling. These deserve your full attention.

We've all done it.

You put on a movie, answer one text, check Instagram for what feels like thirty seconds, look back up—and somehow two characters are divorced, someone has died, and you have absolutely no idea why everyone is suddenly in Norway.

Some movies can survive being background noise.

These are not those movies.

Whether it's because they're visually beautiful, emotionally devastating, slightly unhinged, or simply too good to half-watch, these eight deserve the rarest modern viewing experience of all:

Your full attention.

Past Lives

Quiet movies can sometimes require more attention than loud ones.

Past Lives follows Nora and Hae Sung, childhood friends from South Korea who reconnect years after Nora's family emigrates and eventually spend a few days together in New York.

Nothing about it is particularly explosive. That's the point.

The movie lives in glances, pauses, timing, paths not taken, and the uncomfortable realization that loving the life you have doesn't necessarily erase curiosity about the ones you didn't choose.

Put your phone away or you'll miss half of what the movie is saying without words.

Watch when: you want yearning, beautiful city shots, and an ending that follows you around afterward.

Anatomy of a Fall

A man falls from the upper floor of his home.

His wife is accused of killing him.

Simple enough—until the trial begins exposing an entire marriage.

Anatomy of a Fall works partly as a courtroom mystery, but the real tension comes from something much more interesting: trying to determine whether knowing every ugly detail about a relationship brings you any closer to understanding what really happened inside it.

Arguments are dissected. Private resentment becomes public evidence. Memory becomes unreliable.

You'll change your mind repeatedly.

And yes, you will probably want to discuss the ending immediately.

Watch when: you want something intelligent, tense, and impossible to watch passively.

The Worst Person in the World

Julie doesn't know exactly what she wants.

Unfortunately, adulthood keeps happening anyway.

Over several years in Oslo, The Worst Person in the World follows her through careers, relationships, attraction, indecision, selfishness, grief, and the slow realization that becoming yourself usually looks much messier than people make it seem.

It's romantic without idealizing romance and funny without protecting its characters from consequences.

Most importantly, it understands that sometimes the person making your life complicated is you.

Rude, but accurate.

Watch when: you're questioning your career, your relationship, your life choices, or preferably all three at once.

Aftersun

At first, Aftersun feels almost like someone's home movie.

A young father and his daughter spend a holiday together at a resort in Turkey. They swim. They eat. They joke. They film each other on a camcorder.

Then slowly, almost imperceptibly, the movie changes.

What you're actually watching is memory: an adult daughter looking backward and trying to understand the father she loved before she was old enough to recognize everything he was carrying.

It's tender and understated until suddenly it isn't.

The final stretch has destroyed enough people that it should probably come with complimentary emotional-support snacks.

Watch when: you're prepared to feel perfectly normal for 90 minutes and then strangely haunted for three business days.

Portrait of a Lady on Fire

There are movies about longing.

Then there is Portrait of a Lady on Fire.

Set in 18th-century France, the film follows Marianne, an artist hired to secretly paint the wedding portrait of Héloïse, a young woman promised to a man she doesn't want to marry.

Marianne must study her without revealing why.

Obviously, this arrangement becomes complicated.

Every look matters. Every silence matters. Every tiny detail you notice early returns with greater emotional weight later.

It is controlled, gorgeous, romantic, and quietly brutal.

Watch when: you want a love story where one look across a room does more work than most entire screenplays.

Decision to Leave

Detective story. Romance. Psychological puzzle. Extremely good coats.

Park Chan-wook's Decision to Leave begins when a detective investigating a man's suspicious death becomes increasingly fascinated by the dead man's widow.

From there, attraction and investigation become almost impossible to separate.

The movie is meticulously constructed, visually inventive, seductive, and just confusing enough that looking at your phone for two minutes could be catastrophic.

This is not a laundry-folding movie.

Watch when: you want mystery, obsession, beautiful cinematography, and two people making things considerably harder than necessary.

The Handmaiden

Go into this one knowing as little as possible.

Seriously.

Park Chan-wook's The Handmaiden, inspired by Sarah Waters's novel Fingersmith, begins with an elaborate scheme involving a con man, a wealthy heiress, and the young woman hired to become her maid.

That description barely scratches the surface.

The story keeps shifting perspectives and revealing information that changes what you thought you understood ten minutes earlier.

It's romantic, erotic, funny, dark, gorgeous, occasionally shocking, and constructed like an elaborate little machine.

You will miss something if you're scrolling.

Probably several things.

Watch when: you want twists, schemes, morally questionable people, and impeccable production design.

The Substance

There are subtle movies.

The Substance has respectfully declined.

Demi Moore stars as an aging celebrity offered a mysterious treatment that promises a younger, “better” version of herself. Naturally, the arrangement comes with rules.

Naturally, those rules are not followed.

What begins as a satire of beauty standards, celebrity, aging, and the impossible expectations placed on women gradually transforms into something much stranger, grosser, louder, and more deranged.

By the end, subtlety has left the building completely.

And somehow, that's exactly what makes it work.

Watch when: you want body horror, fashion, female rage, beauty-industry satire, and at least one moment where you audibly say, “What the hell am I watching?”

Phone Down. Lights Low.

The funny thing about having endless entertainment available at all times is that we're getting increasingly good at barely watching any of it.

So pick one.

Put the phone somewhere you can't reach without making it embarrassing.

Turn the lights down.

And give a movie two uninterrupted hours of your attention.

Worst case scenario, you watched a film.

Best case scenario, you find one you can't stop thinking about.
